Mission Statement

Search broke. Gradually, over fifteen years.

Relevance ranking erased source-type distinctions. A search engine optimizing for click-through could put a SEO listicle, a peer-reviewed journal article, and a Wikipedia entry side by side, as long as all three contained the query terms. The structural signals a research librarian would foreground (who produced this, how close to the original evidence, with what editorial process) disappeared from the result page. The user was expected to do the source evaluation after the click. In practice most users did not.

Then ads accelerated the collapse. Every major search engine learned that the most profitable result was not the best answer; it was the one that paid to be there. An entire industry, SEO and content marketing, exists to game the result at the top. Fine when ads are rare and labeled. Not fine when sponsored content outnumbers organic results, when labels shrink each year, and when whole categories (shopping, local, news) become pay-to-play.

Then AI replaced the act of searching. Google's response to ChatGPT was to put AI-generated summaries above every result, replacing the act of searching with the act of accepting an answer. At the same time, the open web filled with AI-generated content farms designed to rank well and serve more ads. The summaries are unreliable. The pages they are built from, now widely called AI slop, are increasingly unreadable. Source evaluation became impossible because the sources themselves were being hidden, blended, or fabricated.

What was lost. Before Google won, AskJeeves and its peers treated a query as a starting point for research, not a request for an authoritative answer. You typed a question. You got a list of pages to read. You decided which to trust. The model assumed the user was an investigator.

AskJeeves launched in 1996, before Google went public. IAC bought it for $1.85 billion in 2005, dropped the Jeeves name in 2006, scaled back search in 2010, and shut down the search engine entirely on May 1, 2026. The farewell read: "Every great search must come to an end." The original idea, that the web could answer a question typed in natural language, was inherited by AI chatbots, the opposite kind of product. The thing Cerulean is trying to be more like just died.

Then the query stopped being yours. Autocomplete narrows what you can type. "Did you mean" rewrites what you asked. Semantic interpretation flattens precise technical language into common phrasing, because common phrasing matches more ad inventory. Each layer is sold as helpful, another tweak to the algorithm. The cumulative effect is that precise language, the primary tool of research, stops working.

Cerulean takes the query you typed.

And then it does what a research librarian would do. Library and information science has spent more than a century building frameworks for distinguishing kinds of sources: the ACRL Framework, the CRAAP test, the BEAM framework, the primary-secondary-tertiary distinction that dates to nineteenth-century historiography. None of it is novel. What is novel is applying it to web search at scale, and surfacing the results to the user instead of hiding them behind relevance ranking.

Cerulean tags every classified result on two axes: source role (how close to the original evidence) and source type (what kind of entity produced it). The methodology page documents the full system, the three-tier classifier pipeline, and the honest limits.

The pitch is structural, not ideological:

  • No ads. Ever. Not commercial placement, not sponsored content, not recommended results.
  • No AI summary. The methodology is the opposite of synthesis: preserve provenance, surface authority signals, trust the reader to evaluate. AI search blends sources and hides them. Cerulean tags them and shows them.
  • Source-role and source-type tags on every classified result. Visible upfront so you can choose what you are looking at before you click.
  • The query you typed. No autocomplete, no rewriting, no semantic flattening.

Text only. Image search and video search are out of scope. Both are dominated by AI-generated content and AI-recommended ranking, with no result-level provenance. A text page can be read, its domain judged, its citations checked. An image or video requires you to view it before you can evaluate it, and even then deepfakes and AI generation make verification fundamentally harder than reading a page.

Dedicated image and video endpoints stay out of scope until result-level provenance is solvable for non-text assets: a working asset-level AI detector, or durable watermark and signature standards at meaningful coverage. Neither exists today. If they do later, the calculus changes. Until then, no.

Video and image links that appear organically in text results, a YouTube clip that genuinely answers a how-to query, a relevant image embedded in an article, are not removed. They are tagged and ranked alongside everything else. What is excluded is a dedicated surface that scrapes video and image platforms as primary sources.

Why humans. Trust in a source isn't authority. It's the editorial process: visible, ongoing scrutiny. Citations you can follow, edit histories you can read, peer review, comment threads, the long tail of people who care enough to argue about a typo. Wikipedia has more of that loop than Britannica does. AI-generated content has none of it. Editorial process is one of the structural signals the library science tradition has always used to evaluate authority, and it is one of the signals Cerulean classifies on.

Cerulean is not better than Google at everything. It is better at exactly one thing: showing you what kind of source you are looking at before you click, by a methodology a research librarian would recognize, with no commercial incentive to lie to you.

If you want what Google used to be, ten blue links, no commentary, this is closer to that.

Tag the source. Trust the reader.
