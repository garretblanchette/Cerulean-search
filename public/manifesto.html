Mission Statement

Search broke. Gradually, over fifteen years.

First, ads. Every major search engine learned that the most profitable result was not the best answer - it was the one that paid to be there. An entire industry - SEO, content marketing - exists to game the result at the top. Fine when ads are rare and labeled. Not fine when sponsored content outnumbers organic results, when labels shrink each year, and when whole categories (shopping, local, news) become pay-to-play.

Then, AI. Google's response to ChatGPT was to put AI-generated summaries above every result, replacing the act of searching with the act of accepting an answer. At the same time, the open web filled with AI-generated content farms designed to rank well and serve more ads. The summaries are unreliable. The pages they are built from - now widely called AI slop - are increasingly unreadable.

What was lost. Before Google won, AskJeeves and its peers treated a query as a starting point for research, not a request for an authoritative answer. You typed a question. You got a list of pages to read. You decided which to trust. The model assumed the user was an investigator.

AskJeeves launched in 1996, before Google went public. IAC bought it for $1.85 billion in 2005, dropped the Jeeves name in 2006, scaled back search in 2010, and shut down the search engine entirely on May 1, 2026. The farewell read: "Every great search must come to an end." The original idea - that the web could answer a question typed in natural language - was inherited by AI chatbots, the opposite kind of product. The thing Cerulean is trying to be more like just died.

Then the query stopped being yours. Autocomplete narrows what you can type. "Did you mean" rewrites what you asked. Semantic interpretation flattens precise technical language into common phrasing, because common phrasing matches more ad inventory. Each layer is sold as helpful, another tweak to the algorithm. The cumulative effect is that precise language - the primary tool of research - stops working.

Cerulean takes the query you typed.

Cerulean is a small protest:

  • No ads. Ever. Not commercial placement, not sponsored content, not recommended results.
  • No AI summary pretending to be the answer. Just sources, ranked.
  • Source-type tags on every result. News, academic, forum, official, commerce, AI-generated - visible upfront so you can choose what you are looking at.
  • AI-content filter, default on. Heuristic estimate of AI-generated pages, hidden by default. Toggle off if you want to see them.

Text only. Image search and video search are out of scope. Both are dominated by AI-generated content and AI-recommended ranking, with no result-level provenance. A text page can be read, its domain judged, its citations checked. An image or video requires you to view it before you can evaluate it, and even then deepfakes and AI generation make verification fundamentally harder than reading a page.

Dedicated image and video endpoints stay out of scope until result-level provenance is solvable for non-text assets - a working asset-level AI detector, or durable watermark and signature standards at meaningful coverage. Neither exists today. If they do later, the calculus changes. Until then, no.

Video and image links that appear organically in text results - a YouTube clip that genuinely answers a how-to query, a relevant image embedded in an article - are not removed. They are tagged and ranked alongside everything else. What is excluded is a dedicated surface that scrapes video and image platforms as primary sources.

Why humans. Trust in a source isn't authority. It's the editorial process: visible, ongoing scrutiny. Citations you can follow, edit histories you can read, peer review, comment threads, the long tail of people who care enough to argue about a typo. Wikipedia has more of that loop than Britannica does. AI-generated content has none of it. Cerulean ranks what humans wrote because human-scrutinized work is the only kind that gets reliably corrected.

It is not better than Google at everything. It is better at exactly one thing: showing you what humans wrote about your query, ranked by signals you can see, with no commercial incentive to lie to you.

If you want what Google used to be - ten blue links, no commentary - this is closer to that.

Don't be Google.
